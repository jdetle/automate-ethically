This is written for someone who has never done this before. If you already
know your way around city hall, skip ahead.

## Step 1: find out who decides

Most of what this movement cares about — cameras, data centers, how a
government office uses software to make decisions — gets decided by one of
three local bodies:

- **City or town council** — most things inside city limits.
- **County board or commission** — things outside city limits, or countywide
  services.
- **School board** — anything involving a school district.

If you're not sure which one applies to your issue, search
"**\[your city\] city council**" or "**\[your county\] board of
commissioners**" and start there. You can always ask — city hall's front desk
or main phone line can point you the right direction.

## Step 2: find the agenda

Every one of these bodies is required to publish its meeting schedule and
agenda in advance, usually on the city or county website under something like
"Agendas & Minutes," "Meetings," or "Government." Search "**\[your city\]
council agenda**" and it's usually the first result.

Look for a line called **"Public Comment"** or **"Citizen Comments."** That's
your window. Some agendas separate "public comment on agenda items" from
"public comment on anything" — either works for this.

## Step 3: sign up to speak

Depending on the town, you'll either:

- Sign a paper sheet when you arrive (arrive 10–15 minutes early), or
- Sign up online in advance through the city's website, or
- Just walk up when the chair calls for public comment — some smaller towns
  don't require sign-up at all.

You'll usually be given **two to three minutes.** A timer or a light is
common. When time's up, wrap up your sentence and sit down — nobody will cut
you off mid-word.

## What it actually feels like

The room is usually much smaller and quieter than you'd expect — often just
the council members, a few staff, and whoever else came to speak. Most
meetings pass with very little public turnout, which is exactly why a handful
of people showing up on one issue tends to stand out. You are not interrupting
anything by being there; public comment exists because they're required to
let you speak.
