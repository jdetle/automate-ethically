## "Won't this kill jobs?"

This isn't a rule about whether automation gets used — it's a rule about
who's accountable when it makes a consequential decision. A company can
still use a camera network, a claims algorithm, a scheduling system. The
ordinance we're asking for just says: when that system's decision affects
someone's life, a named person has to review it and stand behind it. That
doesn't require fewer workers; if anything, it requires someone whose job
is to actually look.

## "Isn't this anti-technology?"

The name is "automate ethically," not "don't automate." Two of our three
pillars are about building better technology, not opposing it — see
[what we build](/about#projects). We think automation is coming whether or
not anyone organizes around it, which is exactly why the accountability
question matters now, while the rules are still being written.

## "Isn't this already the law somewhere?"

In most places, no. Montana's HB 178 is the clearest example of a state
already requiring human review of AI-driven decisions by state agencies —
but that's one state, and it only covers state agencies, not cities,
counties, school boards, or private companies operating in your town. See
the [multistate.ai legislation tracker](https://www.multistate.ai/artificial-intelligence-ai-legislation)
for what's actually on the books where you live.

## "Why start with city councils instead of Congress?"

Because that's where this actually gets decided, and where a handful of
people can still be heard. There are 1,561 AI-related bills moving through
45 state legislatures this session
([multistate.ai](https://www.multistate.ai/artificial-intelligence-ai-legislation),
Mar 2026) — most of that work happens at the state and local level, not in
Washington. Most council meetings pass with almost nobody in the room; a
handful of neighbors speaking on the same issue is a lot of leverage for
very little effort. See [how to find your meeting](/toolkit/find-your-council).

## "Who's behind this? Are you raising money?"

**No, and here's exactly why.** Soliciting political contributions triggers
FEC and state campaign-finance registration requirements — becoming a
registered political committee is a real legal process, not a checkbox, and
we haven't done it. So we don't ask for money, we don't have a donate
button, and if anything on this site ever reads like a fundraising pitch,
that's a bug — [tell us](mailto:join@automate-ethically.com). Right now
this is neighbors, builders, and organizers, working in the open, asking
for time and attention instead. See [the movement](/about) for the
full picture, including the software projects this same principle is
built into.

## "What if my city already does this well?"

Great — say so at the meeting anyway. A council that's already doing the
right thing benefits from public support for keeping it that way, and your
comment becomes part of the record other towns can point to.
